Tesla's rocket Roadster, explained: thrusters, 0–60 in under a second and the physics.

Oct 16, 2026 · 9 min read · by ElectroCalc

Tesla just unveiled a Roadster with SpaceX-style thrusters. Here is what is real, how cold-gas thrusters work, and the force, power and hover maths behind the claims.

Quick answer

Tesla's new Roadster uses compressed-air thrusters, not burning rocket fuel, to help it launch and hover for short bursts. The physics shows why that is needed.

  • 0–100 km/h in 0.81 s needs about 3.5 g, but tyres give only about 1.2 g.
  • That run needs about 857 kW of power but under 0.2 kWh of energy (assumed 1,800 kg).
  • A 600-litre air supply gives only seconds of hover, so expect hops, not flight.

What Tesla showed

On Oct 15, 2026, Tesla revealed its redesigned second-generation Roadster at a SpaceX test site in McGregor, Texas. In the demo, a white two-seat prototype drove up an upside-down ramp using suction, spun in the air and landed with the help of thrusters. Later it jumped over other vehicles while twisting in mid-air. Tesla says production will start in 2028, and no price has been given.

The claim that grabbed headlines is a 0 to 60 mph time of under one second. Reports also quote 0 to 100 km/h in 0.81 seconds. These are manufacturer claims, not independent test results.

It is not a fire-breathing rocket

The thrusters are cold-gas thrusters. They work like a very powerful air nozzle: high-pressure air from tanks is released through a nozzle, and the push comes from the air leaving. There is no fuel and no flame. Tesla says the car uses 10 thrusters fed by about 600 litres of compressed air, stored in carbon-fibre overwrapped pressure vessels of the kind used on SpaceX rockets.

The idea follows Newton's third law: if air goes one way, the car is pushed the other way. Our power guide covers the power and energy basics used below.

Physics 1: why tyres alone can't do it

Acceleration is how fast speed changes. To go from 0 to 100 km/h (27.78 m/s) in 0.81 seconds:

a = Δv ÷ t
Force = mass × acceleration

Acceleration:

a = 27.78 ÷ 0.81 ≈ 34.3 m/s² (about 3.5 g)

Assume a car mass of 1,800 kg (an assumed figure; Tesla has not published one). Force needed:

F = 1,800 × 34.3 ≈ 61.7 kN

Tyres push against the road using friction. With a very grippy friction coefficient of about 1.2 (also an assumption), the most the tyres can deliver is:

F(tyres) = 1.2 × 1,800 × 9.81 ≈ 21.2 kN, which is only about 1.2 g

So about 40 kN must come from somewhere else, such as thrusters and downforce from fans.

Physics 2: the power is the real shock

Moving the car to 100 km/h doesn't take much energy, but it takes it very fast. Energy is kinetic energy (½ × mass × speed²) and power is energy divided by time.

E = ½ × 1,800 × 27.78² ≈ 694 kJ ≈ 0.19 kWh

P = 694 kJ ÷ 0.81 s ≈ 857 kW ≈ 1,150 hp

Power converts to horsepower with our Power Converter (1 hp is about 0.7457 kW).

That is less than 0.2 kWh of energy, but 857 kW of power. If a 100 kWh battery had to supply that, it would be working at 8.6C, which means 8.6 times its capacity per hour. Our BESS guide explains C-rate. It also ignores losses, so the real figure is higher.

Physics 3: how long could it hover?

To hover, the thrust must equal the car's weight. A cold-gas jet's push equals the air mass thrown out each second times its exit speed. Gases of this kind typically leave at roughly 500 to 700 m/s, so let's assume 589 m/s (a specific impulse of about 60 seconds, an illustrative figure). Learn more about specific impulse.

Weight = 1,800 × 9.81 ≈ 17.7 kN

Air needed per second = 17,700 ÷ 589 ≈ 30 kg/s

If the air is stored at about 300 bar (also assumed, since Tesla hasn't said), its density is roughly 357 kg/m³:

30 ÷ 357 ≈ 0.084 m³ = about 84 litres per second

600 litres ÷ 84 L/s ≈ 7 seconds of full hover

The exact numbers depend on values Tesla hasn't shared, but the lesson is solid. Compressed air can give very strong thrust for a very short time, which fits with short hops and stunts, not sustained flight.

Reality check

  • It is a prototype. Production is planned for 2028, and the thrust and rocket versions are limited.
  • Performance numbers are claims. Independent tests haven't happened yet.
  • Critics are sceptical. One investor called the event an engineering flex with little commercial value. Supporters call it a showcase of what is possible.
  • A stunt car is not a street-legal flying car. Regulatory approval for any kind of flight hasn't been confirmed.
  • Safety matters. High-pressure tanks and thrusters raise real engineering and legal questions.

Common mistakes when reading this news

  • Calling it a rocket engine. It uses compressed air, not burning fuel.
  • Mixing up power and energy. The run needs very high power for under a second, but little energy.
  • Treating claims as specs. Wait for independent tests.
  • Assuming it can fly freely. The air supply limits it to short bursts.

Practice problems

Try each one before you open the answer.

1. A car goes from 0 to 100 km/h in 2.0 s. What is its acceleration in g?

27.78 ÷ 2.0 = 13.9 m/s², and 13.9 ÷ 9.81 ≈ 1.4 g.

2. What is the kinetic energy of a 1,500 kg car at 27.78 m/s, in kWh?

½ × 1,500 × 27.78² ≈ 578,700 J, and 578,700 ÷ 3,600,000 ≈ 0.16 kWh.

3. How much thrust is needed to lift a 1,200 kg car?

Weight = 1,200 × 9.81 ≈ 11.8 kN.

Trending terms explained

  • Cold-gas thruster: a nozzle that releases compressed gas to push a vehicle.
  • COPV: a composite overwrapped pressure vessel, a light, strong tank for high-pressure gas.
  • Thrust: the push force, measured in newtons (N) or kilonewtons (kN).
  • g-force: acceleration compared to gravity, where 1 g is 9.81 m/s².
  • Steer-by-wire: steering controlled electronically, with no mechanical link.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Tesla Rocket Roadster?

It is the top version of Tesla's redesigned Roadster. Reports describe it as an invite-only, single-seat model with 10 cold-gas thrusters and what Tesla calls a full flight system.

Is the Tesla Roadster really a rocket?

Not in the usual sense. It uses cold-gas thrusters that release compressed air through nozzles, with no fuel burned.

When will the new Tesla Roadster be available?

Tesla says production will start in 2028. It hasn't announced a price, and the dates and specifications may change.

Can the Tesla Roadster fly?

In the demo it spun and hovered briefly with thrusters and fans, but a few seconds of air supply is very different from sustained flight, and no regulatory approval for flying has been confirmed.

How can a car accelerate faster than tyre grip allows?

Tyres are limited by friction with the road. Thrusters and downforce from fans add extra force, so the car is not limited to tyre grip alone.
