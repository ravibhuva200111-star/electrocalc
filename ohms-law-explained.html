Ohm's Law Explained

Ohm's Law links voltage, current and resistance in one short equation. It is the first tool you reach for when analysing any circuit.

What is Ohm's Law?

In 1827 the German physicist Georg Ohm showed that, for many materials, the current flowing through a conductor is proportional to the voltage applied across it. Double the voltage and the current doubles. Double the resistance and the current halves.

V = I × R

The three forms of the formula

Rearrange the equation depending on what you need to find:

  • Voltage: V = I × R
  • Current: I = V / R
  • Resistance: R = V / I

Memory trick: draw a triangle with V on top and I and R at the bottom. Cover the quantity you want. What remains is the formula: V on top of I × R means V = I × R, and I beside R under a covered V means multiply.

Worked examples

Example 1: find current. A 12 V battery is connected to a 4 Ω resistor.
I = V / R = 12 / 4 = 3 A
Example 2: find voltage. A current of 0.5 A flows through a 220 Ω resistor.
V = I × R = 0.5 × 220 = 110 V
Example 3: find resistance. An LED circuit draws 20 mA with 3 V across the series resistor.
Convert first: 20 mA = 0.02 A. R = V / I = 3 / 0.02 = 150 Ω

Ohm's Law and electrical power

Power is the rate at which energy is used: P = V × I, measured in watts. Substituting Ohm's Law gives two more useful forms:

  • P = I² × R (handy when you know current and resistance)
  • P = V² / R (handy when you know voltage and resistance)

For example, a 10 Ω resistor carrying 2 A dissipates P = 2² × 10 = 40 W. Always check that a resistor's power rating is higher than the power it will dissipate, or it will overheat.

Where Ohm's Law applies, and where it doesn't

Ohm's Law holds for ohmic conductors, where resistance stays constant as voltage changes. Metal wires and standard resistors at stable temperature behave this way. It does not describe diodes, LEDs, transistors or incandescent filaments, whose resistance changes with voltage or temperature. For these you use the component's characteristic curve, though Ohm's Law still works at any single operating point.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting unit conversions. Convert mA to A and kΩ to Ω before calculating.
  • Mixing circuit values. Use the voltage across a component with the current through that same component.
  • Ignoring power ratings. A correct resistance value is not enough if the resistor cannot handle the heat.
  • Applying it to AC without care. In AC circuits with capacitors or inductors, resistance becomes impedance (Z).

Frequently asked questions

What is Ohm's Law?

It states that current through a conductor is directly proportional to the voltage across it and inversely proportional to its resistance: V = I × R.

What are the units?

Volts (V) for voltage, amperes (A) for current and ohms (Ω) for resistance.

Does it work for all components?

No. It applies to ohmic, linear materials at constant temperature. Diodes, transistors and filament lamps are non-ohmic.

How do I calculate power?

Use P = V × I, or P = I² × R, or P = V² / R.
