BESS, explained: how battery energy storage systems work and how to size one.

From rooftop solar backup to grid-scale batteries, learn the parts, the key numbers (MW, MWh, C-rate, efficiency) and worked sizing examples.

What is a BESS?

A Battery Energy Storage System (BESS) stores electricity when it is cheap or plentiful and releases it when it is needed. It is essentially a large, managed battery connected to the grid or to a building, with the electronics and safety systems needed to run it.

Main components

Battery modules and racksThe cells that store the energy, grouped into modules and racks. Lithium iron phosphate (LFP) is a common choice.
BMSThe battery management system monitors cell voltage, current and temperature and protects the cells.
PCS (inverter)The power conversion system converts between the battery's DC and the grid's AC. See our AC vs. DC guide.
EMSThe energy management system decides when to charge and discharge.
Thermal and fire safetyCooling, ventilation and fire detection keep cells in a safe temperature range.
Transformer and switchgearConnect the system to the grid at the right voltage and protect it.

What BESS is used for

  • Solar shifting: store daytime solar and use it in the evening.
  • Peak shaving: discharge during the highest-demand periods to cut peak demand charges.
  • Backup power: keep critical loads running during outages.
  • Grid services: help balance frequency and smooth out renewable output.
  • EV charging support: buffer the grid so fast chargers do not need a large grid connection.

The key numbers: MW, MWh and C-rate

Every BESS has two headline ratings, and they mean different things. Our power guide covers the underlying difference between power and energy.

Duration (h) = MWh / MW
C-rate = MW / MWh

A 1 MW / 2 MWh battery:

Duration = 2 / 1 = 2 hours

C-rate = 1 / 2 = 0.5C

It can deliver its full 1 MW for two hours. A 4-hour system at the same power would need 4 MWh.

Efficiency and depth of discharge

  • Round-trip efficiency: energy out divided by energy in. Lithium-ion systems typically fall around 85–90%.
  • Depth of discharge (DoD): the share of the battery you actually use. Using 90% rather than 100% extends battery life.

Charging with 100 kWh at 88% round-trip efficiency:

Energy out ≈ 100 × 0.88 = 88 kWh

Sizing example 1: home solar backup

You want to supply 15 kWh of evening and night use from stored solar. Assume 90% usable depth of discharge and 92% inverter efficiency.

Battery size = 15 / (0.90 × 0.92) ≈ 18.1 kWh

So you would look for a battery of about 18–20 kWh. See our Battery Life calculator for runtime on a specific load.

Sizing example 2: factory peak shaving

A factory peaks at 500 kW and wants to cap its peak at 400 kW for 2 hours each day.

Power needed = 500 − 400 = 100 kW

Energy delivered = 100 × 2 = 200 kWh

With 90% DoD and 90% efficiency:

Battery size = 200 / (0.90 × 0.90) ≈ 247 kWh, so roughly 250 kWh

C-rate = 100 / 250 = 0.4C (a 2.5-hour battery)

How long will it last?

Cycle life is the number of full charge-discharge cycles before capacity drops to a set level, often 80%. LFP batteries are commonly rated in the thousands of cycles.

A battery rated for 6,000 cycles, used once a day:

Life ≈ 6,000 / 365 ≈ 16 years

Real life depends on temperature, depth of discharge and how hard the battery is used, so treat the figure as a ceiling, not a promise.

Safety and standards

Lithium batteries can overheat if damaged or poorly managed, so BESS design includes temperature monitoring, ventilation, fire detection and suppression, and spacing between units. Installations typically follow recognised standards for energy storage systems and fire-propagation testing, such as UL 9540 and UL 9540A. Always use qualified installers and follow local electrical codes. Cables and breakers must also be sized for the high currents involved, which our Wire Gauge calculator helps with.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up kW and kWh. Power and energy are different, so size for both.
  • Ignoring efficiency and DoD. Nameplate capacity is never fully usable.
  • Sizing only for average load. Peaks decide the power rating you need.
  • Forgetting degradation. Capacity fades over time, so allow headroom.
  • Skipping safety planning. Placement, cooling and fire protection matter as much as the battery.

Practice problems

Try each one before you open the answer.

1. A 2 MW / 4 MWh BESS. What are its duration and C-rate?

Duration = 4 / 2 = 2 hours. C-rate = 2 / 4 = 0.5C.

2. A 100 kWh battery with 90% usable DoD and 90% round-trip efficiency. How much energy can you get out?

100 × 0.90 × 0.90 = 81 kWh.

3. A battery is rated for 4,000 cycles and cycled once a day. About how many years is that?

4,000 / 365 ≈ 11 years.

Frequently asked questions

What does BESS stand for?

BESS stands for Battery Energy Storage System, a setup that stores electrical energy in batteries and releases it when needed, from a home backup unit to a grid-scale installation.

What is the difference between MW and MWh in a BESS?

MW is power, how fast the system can charge or discharge. MWh is energy, how much it can store. A 1 MW / 2 MWh system can deliver 1 MW for two hours.

What is round-trip efficiency?

It is the share of energy you get back out compared with what you put in. Lithium-ion systems typically return roughly 85–90%, so 100 kWh in gives about 85–90 kWh out.

Which battery chemistry do most BESS projects use?

Lithium iron phosphate (LFP) is widely used for stationary storage because of its long cycle life and good thermal stability, though other chemistries are also used.
