How does satellite-to-phone work? Starlink, frequency and wavelength, explained.

Oct 13, 2026 · 8 min read · by ElectroCalc

Your phone may soon connect straight to a satellite. Here is how it works, what frequency and wavelength mean, and the simple maths behind signal delay and loss.

Quick answer

Satellite-to-phone works like a mobile tower in the sky. Two simple formulas explain most of it.

  • Wavelength = 300,000,000 ÷ frequency (Hz)
  • Delay = distance ÷ 300,000 km/s, about 1.8 ms to a satellite 550 km up.
  • The satellite signal is thousands of times weaker than a tower's, so it needs big antennas.

Why everyone is talking about satellite-to-phone

Phone coverage stops at the edge of the last mobile tower. Satellite companies want to remove that edge. This week's tech news covered SpaceX making an $8 billion move toward becoming a nationwide wireless carrier, and Starlink's plans for mobile service. The idea is simple: your normal phone connects to a satellite when there is no tower. Availability depends on your country and operator, and it is being rolled out region by region.

How satellite-to-phone works

A phone talks to a tower using radio waves. A satellite works the same way, except the "tower" is flying overhead. The satellite receives your phone's signal, then passes it down to a ground station that connects to the internet. Services like Starlink use many small satellites in low Earth orbit so that one is almost always overhead.

Frequency and wavelength: the key relationship

Radio waves travel at the speed of light. The frequency (how many waves per second) and the wavelength (the length of one wave) are linked by one simple formula:

Wavelength (m) = 300,000,000 ÷ frequency (Hz)
Frequency = speed of light ÷ wavelength

Higher frequency means shorter wavelength. Our Frequency/Wavelength calculator does this sum for you.

A 1.9 GHz mobile signal (1,900,000,000 Hz):

λ = 300,000,000 ÷ 1,900,000,000 ≈ 0.158 m (15.8 cm)

A 700 MHz signal:

λ = 300,000,000 ÷ 700,000,000 ≈ 0.429 m (42.9 cm)

Why using ordinary phones is hard

A satellite is hundreds of kilometres away, and a signal gets weaker the farther it travels. Engineers calculate this with the free-space path loss formula, in decibels (dB). A bigger number means a weaker signal.

Loss (dB) = 20 log₁₀(distance in km) + 20 log₁₀(frequency in MHz) + 32.44

At 1,900 MHz, comparing a satellite 550 km away with a tower 5 km away:

Satellite: 20 log₁₀(550) + 20 log₁₀(1900) + 32.44 ≈ 153 dB

Tower: 20 log₁₀(5) + 20 log₁₀(1900) + 32.44 ≈ 112 dB

The satellite link is about 41 dB weaker, which is roughly 12,000 times less signal power.

A phone transmits at most about 0.2 W, so this is a big challenge. It is why satellites carry large antennas and why early services began with text messages before moving on to calls and data. See our power guide for how watts work.

Why low orbit is faster than high orbit

Radio waves take time to travel, and distance is what decides how long. Older satellites sit in geostationary orbit, about 35,786 km up. Starlink-style satellites orbit at roughly 550 km.

Delay = distance ÷ speed of light (about 300,000 km/s)

Low orbit, 550 km: 550 ÷ 299,792 ≈ 1.8 ms one way

Geostationary, 35,786 km: 35,786 ÷ 299,792 ≈ 119 ms one way

Going up and back down is double: about 3.7 ms for low orbit and 239 ms for geostationary.

Real internet delay is higher, because of processing and extra links, but low orbit is far better for calls and video.

What to know before you rely on it

  • Availability varies. Check whether your country and operator support it.
  • It needs a clear view of the sky. Trees, buildings and roofs block the signal.
  • Speeds are limited at first. Early services focus on text and basic data.
  • Your phone must support the right bands. Check the manufacturer's information.
  • Battery use can rise. The phone transmits harder to reach a satellite.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up MHz and GHz. 1 GHz is 1,000 MHz.
  • Thinking higher frequency means farther reach. It is usually the opposite.
  • Forgetting distance. Satellites are far away, so the signal is very weak when it arrives.
  • Assuming it works indoors. Walls and roofs block satellite signals.

Practice problems

Try each one before you open the answer.

1. What is the wavelength of a 900 MHz signal?

300,000,000 ÷ 900,000,000 ≈ 0.333 m (33.3 cm).

2. What is the one-way delay for a signal travelling 1,200 km?

1,200 ÷ 299,792 ≈ 0.004 s, or about 4 ms.

3. A signal has a wavelength of 0.1 m. What is its frequency?

300,000,000 ÷ 0.1 = 3,000,000,000 Hz, or 3 GHz.

Trending terms explained

  • LEO: low Earth orbit, a few hundred to about 2,000 km above Earth.
  • Direct-to-cell: satellites that connect straight to ordinary phones.
  • Ground station (gateway): the station that links satellites to the internet.
  • Latency: the delay before data arrives, measured in milliseconds.
  • dB (decibel): a scale for comparing signal strength.

Frequently asked questions

How can my phone connect to a satellite?

Satellite-to-phone services use satellites that act like mobile towers in the sky. The satellite receives your phone's radio signal and passes it to a ground station connected to the internet.

What is the difference between frequency and wavelength?

Frequency is how many wave cycles pass each second, in hertz. Wavelength is the length of one wave. They are linked by wavelength = speed of light ÷ frequency.

Why is low Earth orbit better than geostationary?

It is much closer, so signals arrive in a few milliseconds instead of over 100 milliseconds, and the weaker distance loss helps small phone antennas.

Will it work everywhere and indoors?

It needs a clear view of the sky and usually does not work well indoors. Availability also depends on your country and operator.
