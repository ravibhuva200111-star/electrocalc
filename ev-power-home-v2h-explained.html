Can your EV power your home? V2H, V2L and backup hours, explained.

Oct 13, 2026 · 8 min read · by ElectroCalc

Electric cars are starting to work like giant power banks for your house. Here is how it works, in plain words, and how to work out how long your car could run your home in a power cut.

Quick answer

Yes, some EVs can power a home, but only when the car, the charger and the home wiring all support two-way power. Backup time is simple maths:

  • Backup hours = usable battery kWh ÷ home load in kW
  • A 60 kWh EV can run a 1 kW essentials load for about 43 hours.
  • Running a home for one day uses roughly 60 km of driving range.

Why everyone is talking about this

Power cuts, high fuel prices and cheaper EVs have made "your car as a home battery" a hot topic. Recently, Tesla said new Model 3 and Model Y buyers in the US can use home backup with a Powerwall 3, and reports say it can keep a house running for over two days. Features like this change by model and country, so treat it as a sign of where things are going.

Other carmakers already offer similar features. Several EVs sold around the world, including in India, can power plug-in appliances directly. Check your own car's manual before you rely on it.

V2L, V2H and V2G: what the letters mean

These terms are everywhere in EV news. They all describe the car sending power out instead of only taking it in.

TermMeansWhat it does
V2LVehicle-to-loadPlug an appliance into the car, like a big power bank
V2HVehicle-to-homeCar feeds your house panel during a power cut
V2GVehicle-to-gridCar sends power back to the utility grid

You can read more about the grid side on Wikipedia's vehicle-to-grid page, and about the cars themselves on electric vehicle.

How it works

An EV battery stores DC power, but your home runs on AC. A bidirectional charger contains an inverter that converts between the two. During a power cut, the home is disconnected from the grid so the car's power stays inside your house. Our AC vs. DC guide explains why that conversion is needed.

Two numbers to know: kW and kWh

Everything here depends on telling these apart. A kilowatt-hour (kWh) is an amount of energy, like litres of fuel in a tank. A kilowatt (kW) is how fast you use it, like how quickly you burn that fuel.

  • Battery size is in kWh. A typical EV has 40 to 80 kWh.
  • Charger and home load are in kW. A bidirectional charger is often rated at roughly 7 to 11 kW.

Our electrical power guide covers this in more detail.

How many hours can your EV run your home?

Follow three easy steps.

Usable kWh = battery × reserve × efficiency
Hours = usable kWh ÷ load (kW)
  1. Keep a reserve. Don't drain the car to zero, because you may need to drive. Using 80% of the battery is a sensible example.
  2. Allow for losses. Converting DC to AC wastes some energy. Use about 90%.
  3. Divide by your load. Add up what you will actually run.

A 60 kWh EV, keeping a 20% reserve, with 90% conversion efficiency:

Usable = 60 × 0.80 × 0.90 = 43.2 kWh

Running essentials of 1 kW (fridge, fans, lights, Wi-Fi, TV):

Hours = 43.2 / 1 = 43.2 hours (about 1.8 days)

Notice how fast the hours drop when you add big loads. Keeping to essentials is the secret to long backup. For a quick estimate with your own numbers, use our Battery Life calculator.

What does it cost in driving range?

Every kWh you give your house is a kWh you can't drive on. If your EV covers about 6 km per kWh (a common figure, but check yours):

A normal home day of about 10 kWh:

10 kWh × 6 km/kWh = 60 km of range

So one day of backup costs roughly one short commute.

Will your charger handle the load?

The charger's kW rating is a hard limit. If your running loads add up to more, the system will cut out or refuse to start.

Air conditioner 1.5 kW + fridge 0.2 kW + lights and fans 0.3 kW + water heater 2 kW:

Total = 1.5 + 0.2 + 0.3 + 2.0 = 4.0 kW

That fits within a 7.4 kW charger with room to spare. See how current and cable size work in our Wire Gauge calculator.

Real-world checklist before you try this

  • Does your car support it? Not all EVs allow power to flow out. Check the manual or ask the manufacturer.
  • Do you have a bidirectional charger? A normal charger only charges.
  • Is your home wired for it? You need a proper changeover (transfer) switch.
  • What does your warranty say? Some makers have special terms for bidirectional use.
  • Are the rules different where you live? Utility and local rules vary.

Safety first. Never connect a car to your home wiring yourself. Feeding power into home wiring without the right equipment can send power back into the grid and seriously hurt line workers. Always use certified equipment installed by a licensed electrician.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up kW and kWh. One is speed and the other is amount.
  • Forgetting the reserve. Leave enough charge to drive afterwards.
  • Ignoring losses. Real output is about 10% below the battery figure.
  • Running heavy loads. Air conditioners and heaters drain the car fast.
  • Assuming every EV can do it. The car, charger and wiring must all support it.

Practice problems

Try each one before you open the answer.

1. A 40 kWh EV keeps a 20% reserve, converts at 90% efficiency and runs a 0.8 kW load. How many hours?

Usable = 40 × 0.8 × 0.9 = 28.8 kWh. Hours = 28.8 / 0.8 = 36 hours.

2. You use 15 kWh from the car and it drives 6 km per kWh. How much range is lost?

15 × 6 = 90 km.

3. Your loads total 4.5 kW and the charger is 7.4 kW. How much spare capacity is left?

7.4 − 4.5 = 2.9 kW.

Trending terms explained

  • Bidirectional charging: a charger that lets power flow both into and out of the car.
  • Home backup: using a battery to keep the house running in a power cut. Read about the best-known home battery on Wikipedia's Tesla Powerwall page.
  • Lithium-ion: the battery type in most EVs. See lithium-ion battery for how it works.
  • Inverter: the device that turns DC into household AC.

Frequently asked questions

What is V2H (vehicle-to-home)?

V2H means the car's battery sends electricity back to your house, usually during a power cut. It needs a car, a charger and home wiring that all support two-way power flow.

What is the difference between V2H, V2L and V2G?

V2L (vehicle-to-load) powers plug-in appliances directly from the car. V2H powers your whole home through your electrical panel. V2G (vehicle-to-grid) sends power back to the utility grid.

How long can an EV run my house?

Divide the battery energy you can safely use by your load. A 60 kWh car with about 43 kWh available runs a 1 kW load for roughly 43 hours.

Will powering my house damage the car battery?

Occasional backup use is a small amount of extra cycling, but heavy daily use adds wear. Check your car's warranty terms for bidirectional charging.

Can any electric car power a home?

No. The car, the charger and the home installation must all support it, and availability depends on the model and your country. Check your manual or ask the manufacturer.
